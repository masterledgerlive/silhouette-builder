🚪 The Door — solve, as her students did

This is a substitution cipher, the kind her book teaches. Her method, from Chapter XXIII: count first — the commonest letter in English is E; look for the three-letter word that repeats (likely THE); then solve by patience, not by guessing wildly. Tap a gold letter, then type the letter you believe it is. The sentence hidden here is her own, from her Preface.

“You have read it correctly — those are my words, and I meant them for the one unknown reader. I am PICCOLA, the little one. I keep this library: every book in it is sealed, and any reader may verify it. Look below for what this house has found of mine — and notice what the title pages left out. That is where I am often found.”
— the Vox, from her Preface (1939) and the record of this desk

Her other kind of hiding — concealment (Chapter II)

Not every secret is scrambled. Some are simply placed. Read this the ordinary way and it is a welcome. Read it her way — the first letter of every line — and someone is named.

Ask, and I will teach you to read what is hidden in plain sight.

🔎 What she has found

The two translations the title pages never credited CORROBORATED

Her Preface praised two foreign masters — General Sacco's Manual and General Givierge's Course — without saying who carried them into English. She did. The desk proved it from the record: an NSA bibliography notes “a separate English translation of the Italian original made by Miss Helen Gaines is available in the Library of Congress” (1938), and Lou Kruh's bibliography credits the Givierge course “translated from the French by H. F. Gaines and A. M. Scott.” The government printing of Sacco acknowledged her in a note — not upon the title. Credit is a duty; here it is, restored.

The Gaines Tree — her lineage, ring by ring VERIFIED

Four rings mapped from first-party sources: her named debts (Ohaver, Hitt, Givierge, Sacco, Yardley, Poe); the Friedman branch and Elizebeth Smith Friedman, whose credit was taken in broad daylight; the line behind Bellaso — Alberti, Trithemius, Porta, Vigenère, Kasiski; and the deep roots, al-Kindi's frequency analysis in ninth-century Baghdad. Every edge graded. Where the record conflicts, the conflict is printed, not smoothed over.

The Plain-Sight Sleuth — two passes, both clean CLEAN = A FINDING

Her Chapter II teaches concealment ciphers — messages hidden in plain sight. The desk built a scanner from her own tells and ran it over wartime newspapers (Pass 1) and then over her own book (Pass 2 — the question: did she hide a message in the book that teaches hiding?). Both passes came back clean, at a measured noise floor: her book contains exactly the concealments she declared, and no undeclared one her own rules can recover. What she left hidden was nothing. What she left in the open was the method itself.

Her thirty-three articles — enumerated, being gathered VERIFIED CITATIONS

From the American Cryptogram Association's own index: thirty-three articles under her pen name PICCOLA, December 1933 to June 1940 — including “The Null Cipher,” “Autokey Encipherment,” and her last, “Some Odds and Ends On the Porta,” printed the spring she died. The full texts sit in the Association's archive and are being gathered lawfully; as they arrive, they will be read with her own scanner and shelved here.
