How proof works here

  • Every record is a signed blob. Photos, logs and testimony are hashed, chained into a manifest, and stored on Walrus — recall it and the hash either matches or it doesn't. Nobody can quietly rewrite a pin.
  • Corroboration is automatic, grading is honest. Records at the same place and time cluster together and are graded CORROBORATES, CONTRADICTS, or CONTEXTUALIZES. A pattern is a lead, not a truth, until it survives checking.
  • People stay private. Contributors prove unit and theater with a hashed attestation. Faces, plates and personal details are redacted before anything is stored — personal information never goes onto immutable storage.

The Tribunal — the trial that ended all the other trials

The case they tried first was Dujail, 1982. On July 8, 1982, gunmen of the Iranian-backed al-Dawa party tried to assassinate Saddam Hussein in the Shia town of Dujail, in the middle of the Iran–Iraq War. His reprisal swept up hundreds: around 600 arrested, 148 men and boys executed or dead in custody, families deported, the town's orchards and farmland razed.

Why Dujail matters to the theory: of all the cases against him, Dujail was the one where Saddam had his strongest argument — they tried to kill me first, in wartime. A head of state answering an assassination attempt is the most defensible ground he ever stood on. And it was the smallest case. Behind it in line stood Anfal — the chemical campaigns against the Kurds — the Iran–Iraq War itself, and the crushing of the 1991 Shia uprising. Those trials would have walked straight through the 1980s: the years the United States backed Saddam against Iran, shared intelligence with him, approved dual-use exports, and sent Donald Rumsfeld to shake his hand in Baghdad in 1983 and 1984.

The mechanism (this part is documents, not theory): the Iraqi High Tribunal — created in the U.S.-occupation era and heavily U.S.-funded and supported — tried Dujail first. Saddam was convicted November 5, 2006; his appeal was rejected December 26; and under the tribunal's own statute an upheld death sentence had to be carried out within 30 days, with no stay allowed for other trials. He was hanged December 30, 2006 — four days later. The Anfal trial, already underway, lost its chief defendant mid-stream. The other trials never happened.

The 30-day argument: America doesn't do this. In the United States no one is executed thirty days after a final appeal — capital cases run years of appeals, often well over a decade. And America doesn't destroy its best source: a living Saddam was the highest-value prisoner on earth, and the FBI's own debriefs of him produced real intelligence — including his admission that the WMD posture was a bluff aimed at Iran. The rational design, if justice and intelligence were the goals, was a prison cell, every trial heard, every page extracted. The design chosen was the opposite: execution mandatory within thirty days, no stay, no clemency — a clock whose only certain effect was that the other cases could never be heard.

The result, whatever the motive: we will never hear, under oath and on the record, what the Iran–Iraq War, Anfal, and 1991 files would have shown — including everything they would have shown about Washington's own years backing him. The Gaines Hunt — is anything hidden in the paperwork?

Helen Fouché Gaines wrote the book on finding concealed messages. Her method — frequency, position, structure — is now pointed at this era's own documents: briefing transcripts, category codes, and the calendar itself. Some hiding needs no cipher: try a man on the smallest charge first, make execution mandatory within 30 days, and the bigger trials are never heard. The hunt reports at grade — candidate, corroborated, proven — and a clean pass with zero finds is a result we publish too.
